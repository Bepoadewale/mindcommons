# Letter of Intent evidence notes

**Purpose:** factual research and planning notes for the applicant's own Letter of Intent (LOI). These notes are not application answers. The applicant must choose the argument, examples, wording and AI-use disclosure in their own voice.

**Research checked:** November 19, 2026.

## Evidence-based problem framing

Mozilla's SRHIN host brief identifies an imbalance: mental-health information from African communities may help build AI while meaningful consent, community control over use and a share of benefits are absent. It asks the community to explore community-owned governance, consent, privacy and benefit-sharing tools, with potential pilot work involving young people, people with lived experience and community health workers. This is the host's stated challenge, not an independent prevalence estimate, a finding about every research project, or evidence that SRHIN has endorsed MindCommons.

The RFP also asks applicants to explain who needs the work and how they know. The FAQ says working technology and users are welcome but not required for this individual Fellowship. That flexibility does not turn the absence of MindCommons users or community demand into positive evidence; it remains a real limitation to state plainly and a question for future authorized co-design.

SRHIN's existing work shows a practical mental-health and community-research context. Its 2024 open-access Brave Heart study evaluated a task-shifting intervention with young internally displaced people in Northern Nigeria: 100 people aged 13–24 enrolled and 83 completed post-test; the intervention involved 20 trained community members. The study used a one-group pre-test/post-test design, so it supports a description of SRHIN's community-delivered research experience but should not be presented as a controlled causal estimate or as evidence of demand for a governance product.

SRHIN's September 2026 account of BraveHeart Innovation describes training 10 community leaders and engaging 50 young people in New Kuchingoro and Durumi IDP camps. This is SRHIN's own program report, not an independent evaluation.

Wellcome's 2026 [funded GENSCORE project record](https://wellcome.org/research-funding/funding-portfolio/funded-grants/genscore-project-leveraging-generative-ai-adapt) lists SRHIN staff among grant holders, alongside researchers at the University of Ibadan, Federal Neuro-Psychiatric Hospital Yaba and Imperial College London. It describes plans to develop multilingual depression and anxiety screening in Nigerian Pidgin, Hausa and Yoruba; co-develop a dataset and model; co-design tools with people with lived experience and mental-health experts; and conduct community and clinical validation with a proposed 990 participants. These are aims and planned work in the grant record, not results demonstrated by that page. The record also names ethics, governance, safety and data sovereignty as part of the intended work. Do not frame GENSCORE or SRHIN as lacking community engagement or governance on the basis of the Mozilla challenge.

SRHIN's [A Basic Guide for People with Lived Experience to Get Involved in AI Mental Health Tool Design](https://resources.srhin.org/item/ai-co-design-guide) is a public, plain-language, eight-page guide by SRHIN's Lived Experience Co-Design Team, drawn from GENSCORE and licensed CC BY 4.0. [The PDF is available directly](https://resources.srhin.org/files/ai-co-design-guide.pdf). It describes contributors generating expressions in their own words, reviewing transcriptions, participating in later testing, and asking how their contributions enter the dataset and affect the model. It also flags recorded and transcribed personal material and emotionally difficult sessions, making explanation, support and ongoing contributor feedback practical design questions. This is direct evidence that SRHIN has published guidance for lived-experience involvement in AI mental-health design. It is not an endorsement of MindCommons, a collaboration with the applicant, evidence that GENSCORE processes are inadequate, or evidence of demand for this particular governance prototype.

Together, the grant record and guide show that co-design and responsible AI work are already part of the GENSCORE context. The open question for MindCommons is narrower: whether a portable agreement could make participant- and community-defined requirements enforceable at participating services' processing and output-release boundaries, in a way that complements existing human-led practices. Compatibility and value would need to be established with the host and affected people; there is no existing GENSCORE integration.

The 2026 GENSCORE [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00004) describes a pilot involving 432 Nigerian Pidgin audio responses. It is a preprint, not peer-reviewed evidence. The preprint and Wellcome award record establish an active AI-and-mental-health research context; they do not establish that MindCommons is wanted, nor prove that the recordings were misused or that consent or governance was inadequate.

**Working research question (for the applicant to adapt):** Can rules chosen through a legitimate community process—alongside each person's separate permissions—be translated into portable requirements that participating AI research services check before processing and output release, including agreed benefit conditions? Which rules and decision processes are appropriate is an open question for the host and affected communities, not a decision the developer has already made.

The technical contribution is a synthetic, deployed prototype that lets reviewers inspect this enforcement question. It is not a mental-health service, a real data commons, a community-validated product, an SRHIN project, or evidence of participant demand. Its rules bind only services that implement the agreement; they do not control copied data, model weights or external services that ignore it.

## What the applicant should supply from personal experience

- Two or three specific legal and engineering examples that show relevant judgment and shipped work; describe the applicant's actual role, decisions and outcomes, with verifiable evidence where possible.
- Why this problem matters to the applicant personally, based on the applicant's own experience and reasoning. Do not infer motivation from the prototype or write it on the applicant's behalf.
- What the applicant hopes to learn from an Embedded host and cohort, and how they would respond if community research changes or rejects the current design.
- Accurate traction facts. The demo is publicly deployed, but no community users, feedback, partner commitment, pilot, user count or adoption evidence is claimed. Use no unsupported traffic or impact metric.
- Availability and eligibility details, including the ability to meet the host's time zone expectations and to receive the grant. Confirm these directly in the official form and eligibility rules.

## LOI form checks

The official RFP makes the LOI the primary review stage and asks applicants to link to something reviewers can open. Relevant limits include: public-artifact explanation (200 words); why this host (100); project summary (200); one-liner (30); target users (50); traction (50); impact (150); disruption (50); milestones (150); public benefit (150); selected professional experience (150); technical expertise (100); future role (150); and next steps (150). Check the live form before submission in case it changes.

Mozilla's call lists the deadline as **December 17, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Pacific (UTC−8)**. Its AI-use policy permits supportive work such as brainstorming, clarity and translation, but requires submitted material to reflect the applicant's own original work, ideas and voice. Do not submit these notes as answers. The applicant should disclose assistance accurately.
