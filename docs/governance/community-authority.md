# Community authority and decision legitimacy

This prototype demonstrates software enforcing a recorded rule; it cannot establish that the person or process creating that rule represents a community.

The demonstration uses an explicitly fictional community and a preconfigured synthetic governance scenario. It may show a proposal, review, approval, rejection and supersession path, with an authorization rule for the demo representative. Public demo actions and roles are not evidence of real community consent or authorization.

Any real pilot must co-design, with affected young people, people with lived experience and community health workers, who can propose and decide, what quorum or other legitimacy standards apply, how disagreement and appeals work, what languages and accessible formats are needed, who can pause processing, and how representatives are accountable. A simple majority must not be presumed legitimate. SRHIN is a proposed host from the public RFP; no partnership or endorsement is claimed.

## Governance design questions for co-design

The following are alternatives to discuss after an authorized host relationship exists. They are options, not recommendations or decisions already made for a community.

| Decision                             | Possible arrangements to compare                                                                                                                     | Questions the process must settle                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who may propose a rule?              | Any affected contributor; a selected council; a time-limited working group; a research partner jointly with community members                        | How can people with less connectivity, literacy, time or institutional power place an issue on the agenda?                          |
| Who reviews and decides?             | Delegated representatives with a defined mandate; a community assembly; affected contributor groups; more than one body for different decision types | What makes the mandate legitimate, accessible, informed and accountable? Which changes require renewed consultation?                |
| How are representatives selected?    | Community-defined nomination and selection; rotation; elections where desired; selection by existing trusted processes                               | Whom might each process exclude? How can conflicts of interest, terms and replacement be handled?                                   |
| How can contributors object?         | Private or named objection routes; a supported advocate; group review; a time-limited objection window with accommodations                           | Can a person object without losing unrelated services or being exposed to retaliation? What happens while an objection is assessed? |
| How are disputes addressed?          | Facilitated dialogue; a community-chosen panel; independent safeguarding or ethics referral; staged appeal                                           | Who is trusted to mediate, what records are necessary, and how can a decision be appealed?                                          |
| Who can pause processing?            | Named community delegates; an affected contributor for their own contribution; a safety officer; a multi-party stop mechanism                        | Is the stop immediate, what continues to be blocked, who reviews it, and what is the restart condition?                             |
| How are rules reconsidered?          | Scheduled review; review after a material model/purpose change; contributor-triggered review; time-limited policy with renewal                       | What changes require new individual permission or collective approval? How are old jobs and outputs handled?                        |
| How may benefits change?             | Joint renegotiation; community-only approval of changes; contributor review for affected obligations; escalation when delivery is overdue            | Who may amend a commitment, how is acceptance recorded, and what remedy applies if terms are not met?                               |
| How are representatives accountable? | Public decision rationale; term limits; recall or replacement; conflict disclosures; independent review                                              | What information can be shared safely, how can people challenge a representative, and who hears that challenge?                     |

The prototype can represent a versioned policy state and enforce a recorded decision. It does not implement representative selection, quorum, objection, appeal, safeguarding or pause authority. The browser's switchable fictional seats are a demonstration shortcut and must never be mistaken for authenticated identity or authority. Any future design must remain adaptable if participants reject the developer's initial assumptions.

## Decisions that must remain open until authorized co-design

The developer should not set a real community's decision-makers, voting rule, objection threshold, data categories, permitted purposes, consent language, retention period, benefit allocation, appeal route, or research stop authority before engagement. These are host- and participant-dependent decisions, subject to ethics, legal, privacy and safeguarding review. See the proposed [SRHIN Embedded roadmap](../fellowship/srhin-embedded-roadmap.md) and [decisions reserved for co-design](../fellowship/community-decisions-after-selection.md).

## Prototype boundary

An active policy version can constrain the demo API. It does not bind external researchers, downstream copies, model weights or third-party services. Enforcement exists only inside the participating workflow.
